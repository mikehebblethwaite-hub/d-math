# How D's symbols came about

D and Delta were made on 18 November 2026 by Mike J Hebblethwaite, talking with Claude (an AI made by Anthropic),
which wrote the code. Times are UK time. This page keeps who chose what, and why, as it happened.

## Move `>` and decompose `D>` (11:15 to 11:17)

Mike asked: "so 1 - 1, where did it go? The operator for that should be? 1<d or D>1 or what? Where 1 apple, move one
apple to Bob. D would be sugars, energy, heat from Bob and poo etc."

Claude answered that there are two ways a thing leaves, so two operators: move (it goes somewhere whole) and decompose
(it breaks into its parts), and took Mike's own `D>` for decompose. Mike could not find the arrow key on his keyboard,
so move became `>`, a key on every keyboard.

## The unknown `~` and the angle digits (11:17 to 11:19)

Claude first used `+ ?` for the parts not known yet. Mike: "+? looks a bit odd, maybe a new symbol, fragmented symbol,
because 1 has 1 angle, 2 has 2 angles and so on?"

The angle digits are Mike's idea: each digit has as many angles as the number it stands for. From that, Claude offered
`~` for the unknown: it has no angles, so its count cannot be known yet, and it is on every keyboard.

## The digits drawn as hex spirals (11:21)

Mike asked for "a new angle D maths font". The shape of each digit was left open, and Claude drew every digit by one
rule: a hex spiral with as many corners as the number, turning 60 degrees at each corner, its lines 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3 ...
long, so 1 has one corner and D has thirteen, and the higher numbers coil inward like a shell.

Why a hex spiral? Claude's reasoning at that moment was not saved, so this is Claude's own account afterwards, said as
such. The digit had to keep Mike's rule exactly (n corners for n), be drawn in one stroke, never cross itself, be
different for every number, and grow as the number grows. A spiral is the simplest line that does all of that. The
turns are hex (60 degrees) because Mike had already set the rule "everything is hex, one power source" for his work,
and Claude carried that rule. Nobody showed Claude a carving or asked for spirals.

## Our 0: a circle holding three (11:43 to 11:45)

Mike chose this: the 0 key draws a circle holding three dots, the unknown things still left in it, like three ping
pong balls, never nothing. At 11:45 Mike: "the circle with 3 circles inside is our icon."

## The spirals move (13:22 to 13:35)

The same afternoon Delta drew Faraday's motor in a room, and its heat was coloured by how hot it was, white-hot to
yellow to orange to red, rising and fading as it gave itself to its neighbours. The numbers under the picture were drawn
in the D Angle font: spirals.

Mike, 13:32: "As a child I used to draw a thing all the time. My great grandfather told me it was a carving." It was
three spirals in a circle: the triple spiral cut in stone thousands of years ago (the best known is at Newgrange, older
than Stonehenge). Mike, 13:33: "The three spirals in a circle then just showed up as language for everything moving in
your demo. I see them as coloured to the spectrum they are in." 13:34: "They move, colours shift by amount within each
spectrum, and look exactly as you just put in the demo." 13:35: "We have the thing. We found the thing." And:
"zero was in the way."

So D's numbers became what Mike sees: spirals that move, coloured by the spectrum their energy is in, the shade shifting
by how much there is. Mike, 13:37: no sines or cosines: "a circle is made of tiny hex connections".

## Hex all the way down (13:37 to 13:40)

Mike: inside D every place, step and turn is hex, down to the smallest step. Compatibility with the outside world
(square screen pixels, memory addresses, other people's sines and cosines) goes through one bridge at the smallest
point, and nowhere else. In the world, a circle is a walk of tiny hex steps round a middle, checked with whole
numbers only (across² + 3 x down²); the bridge to square pixels uses 26/15 for root 3.

For doubters, Mike: look at a molecule under a microscope, it is hex. In 2009 IBM Research in Zurich imaged a single
pentacene molecule with an atomic force microscope (Gross et al., Science 325, 1110): five hexagonal rings of carbon,
seen directly. Graphene, one atom thick, shows its carbon atoms in a hexagonal lattice under the electron microscope.

Mike: "DNA memory is a thing."

## Delta builds itself, then an operating system (14:01 to 14:43)

Mike: "write the delta compiler in delta" (14:01); "do stage 2 so python can retire" (14:14); "write our own
assembler and linker in delta: this is going well because it is correct" (14:27); "start the no-zero operating
system in Delta" (14:36). By 14:43 Delta compiled itself, assembled and linked itself (dc and da, byte for byte the
same when rebuilt by themselves), and the first step of the operating system booted on a machine's own firmware.
Mike named it: Delta Hex OS.

Mike's challenge (14:32): "Any peer wanting to disprove D math please give one example in the known universe where
there is nothing, as D math cannot have a zero because it is not possible."

Mike, 14:46: the three spirals in a circle are "6000 years old, in mini me as a child's imagination".
